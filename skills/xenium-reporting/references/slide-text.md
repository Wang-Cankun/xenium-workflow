# Slide text

Read when writing or revising titles, take-home lines, bullets, source credits or
speaker cues. [Presentation production](presentation.md) owns the whole-talk account,
display selection and revision process.

## Title and point

Default to a short topic title for navigation and one bold take-home line that states
the page's point. Use conclusion titles when requested; avoid repeating the same
sentence below them. A cover identifies the topic and occasion. Preserve title
hierarchy and sizing in the template profile.

The take-home line says what the audience should notice or understand, at the strength
the evidence supports. "Similar density" is narrower than "consistent quality" when
other measurements differ. Read the take-home lines in order: together with the
displays, they should carry the account.

## Concrete wording

Name the object, action and relevant consequence. Replace broad statements about
reliability, evidence or usefulness with the decision this page explains. For example,
"Different kinds of information need different homes" becomes "Keep run results in
the project record so the next session can continue." The display must then show
that record or relationship. Use the example's vocabulary rather than a new abstract
label for each page; define a technical term when the audience needs it.

## Visible findings

On a result page, write findings a viewer can locate in that page's display. Point to
the relevant panel, group or comparison and give the implication when it is not
obvious. "Sections within each group vary widely" is a finding; "each dot is a
section" is a reading cue. Both can help, but the cue does not replace the finding.

A fact from another table, log or unplotted subgroup needs its own displayed evidence
or belongs in the cues/supporting material. The presenter should be able to locate
the evidence if asked. A schematic supports an explanation of relationships; it does
not establish a measured improvement.

For a method, include the missing premise that makes the choice intelligible. Explain
why a step is needed when the audience could reasonably ask "why not just use the
next step?" Check that explanation against the source. Motivation can emerge through
an example and transition; it need not become a repeated "Why it matters" heading.

## Reading load and emphasis

Use short phrases and only the numbers the point needs, with counting units. Count
words inside the figure as well as editable text. Split ideas or remove secondary
detail before reducing size. Keep one clear emphasis. When a take-home line, diagram
caption and bottom summary repeat the same point, retain the strongest version and
use the other space for the example. A table should make a comparison easy to scan;
move paragraph-length explanations into cues or split the explanation across pages.
Check whether listeners can follow the display while hearing the planned cues.

Use one term per concept and introduce it before the first dependent slide. Say the
content directly; omit headings that merely announce a purpose or rationale. Use
commas, semicolons or line breaks for lists rather than middle-dot separators.

## Qualifications and sources

Write a claim precisely enough to remain true without a defensive footnote. A limit
that changes its meaning belongs in the wording or display, such as "pilot regions"
in the result label. Put background caveats, technical definitions not needed to read
the display, and internal provenance in the cues or supporting material.

Reserve the footer for necessary direct source URLs, in the template's small source
style. Honor an explicit source-size request in points. Internal paths, filenames and
"supplied repository" provenance go in notes or the source record, including when
the builder offers a source-footer field. Leave that footer empty when no audience-
useful source URL is needed. Cite borrowed evidence on its page; keep full citations
separately. A circulated copy must retain the context needed to interpret its claims
after its notes are removed.

## Cues and questions

Write glanceable speaking cues, usually a few short lines: the point, how to read the
display, the essential explanation or qualification, and the transition. Use English
unless requested otherwise. Keep extended answers in a Q&A companion and citations
apart from cues. Honor a request for a full script.

An open-questions page asks what blocks the next useful step, with related asks
combined. Phrase requests from the presenter's actual role and make them answerable
by people in the room.

## Completion

Inspect the rendered words, including diagram labels, captions and footers:

- The point names a specific observation or decision that the display makes visible.
- Each repeated statement has a distinct purpose; secondary explanations belong in
  cues. Labels and tables can be read alongside the planned spoken explanation.
- Terms are introduced, claims retain necessary scope, and source footers contain
  only the necessary direct URLs.

Revise any violation before delivery, then read the sequence for continuity. Apply
general corrections across the deck through the revision step in
[presentation production](presentation.md).
